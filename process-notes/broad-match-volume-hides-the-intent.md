# A broad-match export sorted by volume tells you nothing until you ask what the volume is made of

**Class:** keyword research false positive
**Written:** 25 August 2026

## What happened

A broad-match keyword export for a mythological subject came back with twenty
terms, most of them above 10,000 searches a month, several marked Easy. Sorted by
volume it looked like an obvious, high-demand, low-difficulty opportunity.

Reading the terms rather than the numbers:

- `<subject> 2026`, `<subject> film`, `<subject> movie`, `cast of <subject>`,
  `<subject> budget`, `<subject> teaser`, `<subject> release date`,
  `<lead actor> <subject>` — a major film adaptation, mid release cycle
- another Easy, >10,000 term turned out to be a **1992 animated adaptation**
- another was a **television serial**
- the bare head term's page one was **Wikipedia, Britannica and IMDb**

**Of twenty terms, exactly one carried the informational intent the client's
content actually serves.** That one was a question phrase, Easy, above 10,000, and
would have been overlooked by anyone reading the list top-down by volume.

## Why it is dangerous rather than merely unhelpful

Contaminated intent is worse than low volume, in three ways.

1. **It cannot be won.** Entertainment queries return entertainment SERPs. An
   explainer does not displace IMDb for a film query no matter how good it is.
2. **Winning is worse than losing.** Impressions rise, click-through collapses, and
   the site's own analytics now contain a false signal about what its audience
   wants.
3. **It survives review.** A volume table looks like evidence. Handing a client
   "10,000/month, Low competition" is persuasive and, when the volume belongs to a
   film release, wrong.

A related trap: a film or show release makes the contamination **temporary and
directional**. The same export next year may look completely different. Date every
capture and re-check before reusing it.

## Rule

1. **Never recommend a term from a volume table alone.** Read every term and label
   what the searcher wants.
2. **Look for a release.** A film, series, game, book or product sharing the name
   will dominate a mythological, historical or generic head term while it is in
   cycle. Check the current year alongside the term.
3. **Open the SERP for the head term before proposing it.** If page one is
   encyclopedias, IMDb, or retail, the question is closed regardless of the KD
   score.
4. **Weigh the target against the site's current standing.** A site drawing a few
   hundred impressions a month does not enter a Wikipedia-and-Britannica page one.
   Say so plainly rather than proposing it.
5. **The question phrases are usually the prize.** "who wrote X", "where is X",
   "how was X built" carry informational intent by construction, and are where a
   genuinely useful page can win. In this case one such term matched the client's
   existing research exactly.

## The general form

> Volume measures how many people type a string. It does not measure how many of
> them want what you have. Sorting by volume ranks strings, not opportunities.
